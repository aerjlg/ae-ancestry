Descendants

Census & register checklist

Every person is shown against the returns they could appear in, worked out from their birth and death years. Tap a cell to cycle it: blank → found → searched, nothing there → blank. When you mark one found you can add the reference (piece/folio/page, or a household URL).
A fact without a citation is a rumour. Record where you found it, precisely enough that you could find it again in ten years.
Negative results are worth as much as positive ones — they stop you searching the same reel twice.

Next steps

Save your work

Downloading writes every record into a fresh copy of this page, so the file itself is the archive — move it to Google Drive and it still opens with everything intact. The browser also keeps a working copy as you go, but only the downloaded file survives a change of machine.

Import research findings

For a small file someone has sent you — new people, a source, a log entry — that should be added to what's already here, not replace it. New people are linked to existing family by matching names. If a name repeats down the generations (six Thomas Greens is a real problem, not a hypothetical one), a birth year in brackets narrows it — "Thomas Green (1802)" — and if it still can't be settled automatically you'll get a pick-list before anything is saved wrong.

Dates you'll keep needing

Civil registration begins 1 July 1837 in England & Wales — before that, parish registers are the record.
Census nights: 1841, 1851, 1861, 1871, 1881, 1891, 1901, 1911, 1921. The 1931 returns burned in 1942 and no census was taken in 1941; the 1939 Register fills the gap.
The 1841 census rounds adult ages down to the nearest five and only asks whether a person was born in the county — treat both loosely.
Rose's Act of 1812 standardised parish register forms, so entries before then carry far less detail.
Marriage certificates give the father's name and occupation — the single most useful document for climbing a generation.
